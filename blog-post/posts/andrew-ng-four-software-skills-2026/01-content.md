# Four Software Skills That Actually Matter in 2026

You used to be the person who laid perfect bricks. Straight lines, even mortar, no wasted motion. That was the job, and you were good at it.

Now there's a robot that lays bricks faster than you ever could. It doesn't get tired. It doesn't need a break. It just lays bricks.

So what's left for you?

## The problem: everyone's optimizing the wrong skill

Most people reacting to AI are doing one of two things. Either panicking that coding is dead, or doubling down on writing code faster, cleaner, more of it. Both miss the point.

Andrew Ng looked at this properly. He analyzed 10,000 job postings to figure out which software skills actually matter now, and coding wasn't one of the four standout answers. Not because it stopped mattering. Because it became the floor, not the ceiling. It's the prerequisite underneath everything else, not the thing that makes you valuable anymore.

If you're still measuring your worth by how fast you can type out a function, you're optimizing for a skill that's being commoditized in real time.

## The reframe: you're not the bricklayer anymore

Here's the shift. You're not the one laying bricks. You're the one deciding where the wall goes, why it needs to be there, and whether it should be a wall at all.

Laborer to architect. That's the whole move. And it changes what "good at your job" means.

## The four skills that replace "writes good code"

### 1. Building and deploying AI applications

This sounds obvious until you sit with the real point. Normal software is predictable: same input, same output, every time. AI is not. Ask a model the same question twice and you can get two different answers.

The skill isn't "I can call an API and wire up a chatbot." The skill is controlling that unpredictability. That means disciplined evaluation, systematic error analysis, and actually measuring whether your AI system is getting better or just looking better in a demo. Shipping a demo is easy. Shipping something that holds up when a real user pushes on it is the actual job.

### 2. Software engineering fundamentals, arguably more than before

The assumption goes: if AI is writing the code, fundamentals don't matter anymore. That's backwards.

Every software decision is a tradeoff. Fast versus secure versus cheap. You can tell an AI "make it fast, secure, and cheap" in one sentence, but you won't know which of those it quietly deprioritized on your behalf, or what that costs you six months from now, unless you understand the fundamentals well enough to catch the compromise before it ships.

Without that grounding, you find out about the tradeoff the same way you find out about most bad decisions: in production, at the worst possible time.

### 3. Using coding agents well

Knowing what an agent is good at, where it breaks down, and how much to trust its output unsupervised. One careless instruction to an agent with the wrong scope can take down a production database. That's not hypothetical caution, that's the actual failure mode.

And there's a second half to this skill that gets ignored: efficiency. How much time and how many tokens you burn to get something done matters as much as whether you got it done at all. An agent that solves the problem in 40 expensive turns when it could have taken 5 well-scoped ones isn't actually a win.

### 4. Shaping what gets built

This is the one people skip past fastest, and it's the one that separates architects from very fast laborers.

You're no longer just executing tickets someone else wrote. Part of the job now is deciding which problem is even worth solving in the first place. Anyone, including an AI, can build the thing you ask for. Far fewer people can tell you whether you asked for the right thing.

## What this actually changes day to day

**Evaluation becomes a first-class skill, not an afterthought.** If you're not measuring whether your AI feature is actually working, you don't know if you're improving it or just changing it.

**Fundamentals stop being "nice to have for interviews."** They become the thing that lets you audit an AI's decisions instead of trusting them blindly.

**Trust in agents becomes calibrated, not binary.** You're not "using AI" or "not using AI." You're deciding, task by task, how much rope to give it.

**Taste becomes a technical skill.** Deciding what's worth building isn't a product manager's job you tolerate. It's now squarely yours too.

## When this framework actually matters

- When you're deciding what to learn next and coding bootcamps aren't cutting it anymore
- When you're mentoring someone junior and they think "learn to prompt" is the whole plan
- When you're the one approving what an agent just shipped, and you need a reason beyond "it looked fine"
- When you're setting a roadmap and the real question isn't "can we build this" but "should we"

## Try this now

Pick one feature you shipped recently with AI in the loop somewhere. Ask yourself honestly: did you evaluate it, or did you just look at it once and move on? If the answer is the second one, that's the gap. Start there.
