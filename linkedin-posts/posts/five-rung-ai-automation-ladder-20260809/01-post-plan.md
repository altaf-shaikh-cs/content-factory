## Core Insight

AI automation is not a flat capability question, it is a five-rung ladder (prompt, skill, loop, routine, agent team), and a rung only holds if the rung below it is already reliable, which is why people who jump to agent teams end up doing rung-1 work with a bigger bill.

## Target Audience

Early-to-mid career engineers in Indian tech hubs (Mumbai, Bengaluru, Delhi, Hyderabad) who have already used AI coding tools daily for a year, have tried building an agent or automation, and watched it work brilliantly once and then quietly stop being trustworthy. Senior engineers who are being asked to "add agents" to their team's workflow and do not have a way to sequence it. Not AI-curious beginners, and not researchers.

## Structure

1. The failure everyone recognizes: the automation worked on day one, then you became its full-time babysitter. That is not a model problem, it is a rung problem.
2. Name the ladder: prompt, skill, loop, routine, agent team. One line each, defined by how much of the work you have actually handed over, not by how impressive it sounds.
3. The load-bearing rule: a rung only holds if the one under it is reliable. Get one manual run right, save it as a skill, wrap it in a loop with a verifier and a stop condition, and only then put it on a schedule.
4. What skipping costs, from real builds: shipped UI where no feature worked because nothing verified the flow, so the human became the verifier screen by screen. The tool that "won" a comparison did not write better code, it opened the browser and checked itself.
5. The diagnostic: which rung you are actually on, and the single next move for each. Plus the honest cost: every rung you climb is maintenance you cannot walk away from, so do not climb higher than the work needs.

## CTA

Ask which rung they are actually on, and specifically which one they tried to skip. Frame it as a self-check, not a poll: "Be honest about the rung you are on, not the one you talk about. Which one did you try to skip?"

## Tone Range

Educational with a confessional edge. Authoritative on the framework, honest about the cost. No guru voice, no hype, no "here is the secret."

## Image Brief

Format: document carousel (multi-slide deck), 8 slides, plus a caption.

Slide-by-slide content the deck must carry:
1. Hook slide: "Your AI automation didn't fail. You skipped a rung." Large type, high contrast.
2. The ladder, all five rungs visible at once, labelled 1 to 5: Prompt, Skill, Loop, Routine, Agent team. This is the saveable slide.
3. Rung 1 and 2 detail: Prompt (you are the memory, planner and checker) vs Skill (saved once, applied every time). One-line "what you own" per rung.
4. Rung 3 detail: Loop. The three parts a prompt does not have: verifier, state, stop condition. Callout: without a real check you do not have a loop, you have the agent agreeing with itself.
5. Rung 4 and 5 detail: Routine (the trigger stops being you) and Agent team (split the maker from the checker).
6. The rule slide: a rung only holds if the one below it is reliable. Show the correct order: one reliable manual run, then skill, then loop with a gate, then schedule.
7. The diagnostic slide: 5 lines, "if you <symptom>, you are on rung N, your next move is <X>." This is the second saveable slide.
8. Cost + CTA slide: every rung is maintenance you cannot walk away from, so do not climb higher than the work needs. Then the CTA question.

Contrast pairs (no invented numbers, the library has no stats for this idea):
  - Rung 1: you are the verifier / Rung 3: something else is
  - Ambition rung 5 / machinery rung 1
  - "Should work" / verified
  - Prompt: close the tab and it is gone / Routine: closes your laptop and it keeps going

Tagline: "A rung only holds if the one below it is reliable."

Hard constraint: no emojis, no logos, no company names, no invented statistics.

## Performance Context

- Best performing angle so far: Contrarian at 7.43% (1 post), Story-first at 6.39% avg (2 posts). Bold claim-first is variable at 3.86% avg across 3 posts.
- Hook styles that drove comments: a specific uncomfortable truth stated as an indictment. "I wasn't learning Gen AI. I was consuming it." Reframing a belief the audience already holds.
- Hook styles that drove passive reactions only: scene-setting openers, and abstract "I built a system" process posts. The routines post got the highest impressions in its period (848) and the second-worst engagement rate (2.48%) because the hook was a scene, not a claim.
- Topics/themes that resonated: practical skill-building and roadmap content is the single strongest category (gen-ai-roadmap, 7.69%, top performer). Audience saves and engages with things that let them locate themselves on a path.
- What to avoid repeating: process-narration about the author's own system without a reader-side payoff. External links in the body (move to first comment). Slow scene openers. Generic hashtag clusters, fewer and tighter outperformed.
- Implication for THIS post: this is the same shape as the top performer, a path the reader can locate themselves on, so the diagnostic must be genuinely usable and not decorative. The hook must be an indictment ("you skipped a rung"), not a scene. Keep the author's own system as evidence only, never as the subject. This is the account's first document carousel, so there is no format baseline, judge it on engagement rate against 4.0%.

## Angle Assignments

Fixed angles (always used):
  - Angle A: Story-first — open with the moment the shipped UI had working screens and no working features, and the realisation that the human had quietly become the verifier
  - Angle B: Bold claim-first — open with "you are not running an agent team, you are doing rung-1 work with a bigger bill"

N=3. Angle C: **Tactical/how-to-first** — lead with the diagnostic itself, five symptoms and the single next move for each, then name the ladder underneath it.

Chose Tactical/how-to-first because decision rule 2 matched: the idea's centre of gravity is a concrete, repeatable diagnostic plus the 4-question test for whether to climb at all. Rule 1 (vulnerability/confession) technically matches on the skipped-rungs reflection, but that material is already load-bearing for Angle A's story open, so a confession-first variant would duplicate A rather than produce something A and B structurally cannot. Rule 2 is the first rule that yields distinct material: a reader-usable checklist that neither the story open nor the claim open can lead with.
